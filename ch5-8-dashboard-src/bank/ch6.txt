Q: $X$ has p.d.f. $f(x)=3x^{-4},\ x\ge1$ (zero otherwise). Which moments of $X$ exist?
A: All moments
B: Only $E(X)$ and $E(X^2)$
C: Only $E(X)$
D: None
ANS: B
S: $E(X^r)=\int_1^\infty 3x^{r-4}dx$ converges iff $r-4<-1$, i.e. $r<3$.
S: $E(X)=\tfrac32$, $E(X^2)=3$ exist; $E(X^3)=\int_1^\infty 3x^{-1}dx=\infty$.
S: So mean and variance exist ($\text{Var}=3-\tfrac94=\tfrac34$) but no moment of order $\ge3$.
SC: Pareto index $\alpha$ (density $\propto x^{-\alpha-1}$): $E(X^r)<\infty\iff r<\alpha$. Here $\alpha=3$.
TR: Option C would be the answer for $f(x)=2x^{-3}$ ($\alpha=2$).
CHK: integrate(3*x**(-3),(x,1,oo)) == R(3,2)
CHK: integrate(3*x**(-2),(x,1,oo)) == 3
TIP: Existence test for power tails: $\int_1^\infty x^{r}\,x^{-(\alpha+1)}dx<\infty\iff r<\alpha$.
TIP: Here $\text{Var}(X)=3-\left(\tfrac32\right)^2=\tfrac34$ — computable because $r=2<3$.

Q: If $E(X^4)$ exists, consider:<br>1. $E|X|^3$ exists<br>2. $E(X^2)$ exists<br>3. $E(X^5)$ exists<br>Which are necessarily true?
A: 1 and 2 only
B: 2 only
C: 1, 2 and 3
D: 2 and 3 only
ANS: A
S: Theorem 6·7: if $E|X|^r<\infty$ then $E|X|^s<\infty$ for every $0<s\le r$.
S: Reason: $|x|^s\le1+|x|^r$ for all $x$ when $s\le r$.
S: Higher moments need not exist — e.g. Pareto with index $4.5$ has $E(X^4)<\infty$ but $E(X^5)=\infty$.
SC: Moments exist "downwards", never automatically "upwards".
TIP: Inequality behind it: $|x|^s\le1+|x|^r$ for $0<s\le r$, so a finite $r$-th moment dominates every lower one.
TIP: Lyapunov gives more: $\left(E|X|^s\right)^{1/s}\le\left(E|X|^r\right)^{1/r}$.
TIP: The converse fails: the $t$-distribution with 5 d.f. has $E(X^4)<\infty$ but $E|X|^5=\infty$.

Q: Consider the following for random variables with the relevant expectations finite:<br>1. $|E(X)|\le E|X|$<br>2. For $X>0$: $E(1/X)\ge 1/E(X)$<br>3. $[E(XY)]^2\le E(X^2)E(Y^2)$<br>Which are correct?
A: 1 and 2 only
B: 1 and 3 only
C: 2 and 3 only
D: 1, 2 and 3
ANS: D
S: (1) $-|X|\le X\le|X|$; take expectations (Theorem 6·6).
S: (2) $g(x)=1/x$ is convex on $(0,\infty)$; Jensen gives $E\,g(X)\ge g(EX)$. Equality iff $X$ is degenerate.
S: (3) Cauchy–Schwarz (Theorem 6·11): $E(X-\lambda Y)^2\ge0$ for all $\lambda$ gives a non-positive discriminant.
SC: Jensen for convex $g$: $E\,g(X)\ge g(EX)$. Instances: $E X^2\ge(EX)^2$, $E e^X\ge e^{EX}$, $E(1/X)\ge1/EX$, $E\ln X\le\ln EX$ (concave).
TIP: Jensen's direction: convex function outside the expectation is smaller — $g(EX)\le E\,g(X)$.
TIP: Cauchy–Schwarz with $Y\equiv1$ gives $[E(X)]^2\le E(X^2)$, i.e. $\text{Var}(X)\ge0$.
TIP: Equality in Cauchy–Schwarz holds iff $Y=cX$ with probability 1 — the reason $|\rho|=1$ means a linear relation.

Q: For a continuous random variable $X$ with finite mean, $E|X-c|$ is minimum when $c$ equals the
A: mean
B: median
C: mode
D: mid-range
ANS: B
S: $\dfrac{d}{dc}E|X-c|=\dfrac{d}{dc}\left[\int_{-\infty}^c(c-x)f\,dx+\int_c^\infty(x-c)f\,dx\right]=F(c)-[1-F(c)]$.
S: Setting this to $0$: $F(c)=\tfrac12$, i.e. $c=$ median. The second derivative $2f(c)\ge0$ confirms a minimum.
SC: Squared loss $\to$ mean; absolute loss $\to$ median; 0–1 loss $\to$ mode.
TR: $E(X-c)^2$ is minimised at the mean — do not mix the two.
TIP: Median minimises the sum of absolute deviations for a data set too (G&K Example 5·19).
TIP: If the median is not unique (flat $F$), every point of the median interval minimises $E|X-c|$.

Q: A non-negative integer-valued random variable $X$ satisfies $P(X>n)=\dfrac1{(n+1)^2}$, $n=0,1,2,\dots$. Then $E(X)$ equals
A: $\dfrac{\pi^2}6$
B: $1$
C: $\infty$
D: $\dfrac{\pi^2}{12}$

TIP: $\sum1/n^2=\pi^2/6\approx1.645$ — a standard constant worth memorising.
TIP: The p.m.f. is $P(X=n)=\dfrac1{n^2}-\dfrac1{(n+1)^2}$ for $n\ge1$.

Q: A non-negative random variable has survival function $P(X>x)=\dfrac1{(1+x)^2}$, $x\ge0$. Its mean is
A: $1$
B: $\tfrac12$
C: $2$
D: $\infty$
ANS: A
S: $E(X)=\int_0^\infty P(X>x)\,dx=\int_0^\infty(1+x)^{-2}dx=\left[-\tfrac1{1+x}\right]_0^\infty=1$.

Q: For a random variable $X$ taking values $0,1,2,\dots$ with $E(X^2)<\infty$, the sum $\displaystyle\sum_{x=0}^{\infty}(2x+1)P(X>x)$ equals
A: $E(X^2)$
B: $E(X)$
C: $E[X(X-1)]$
D: $\text{Var}(X)$
ANS: A
S: $\sum_{x\ge0}(2x+1)\sum_{k>x}p_k=\sum_k p_k\sum_{x=0}^{k-1}(2x+1)$ (interchange the sums).
S: $\sum_{x=0}^{k-1}(2x+1)=k^2$ (sum of the first $k$ odd numbers).
S: Hence the sum $=\sum_k k^2p_k=E(X^2)$.
SC: General tail-sum: $E[g(X)]=g(0)+\sum_{x\ge0}[g(x+1)-g(x)]P(X>x)$. With $g(x)=x^2$ the increments are $2x+1$; with $g(x)=x(x-1)$ they are $2x$ (the 2023 PYQ form).
CHK: (lambda L: sum((2*xx+1)*sum(L[j] for j in range(xx+1,4)) for xx in range(4)) == sum(j**2*L[j] for j in range(4)))([R(1,10),R(2,10),R(3,10),R(4,10)])
TIP: Swap the order of summation: $\sum_x\sum_{k>x}=\sum_k\sum_{x<k}$ — the standard trick in tail-sum proofs.
TIP: Replace $(2x+1)$ by $1$: you get $E(X)$; by $2x$: you get $E[X(X-1)]$.
TIP: Continuous analogue: $E(X^2)=\int_0^\infty2x\,P(X>x)\,dx$.

Q: $X$ and $Y$ are independent and identically distributed positive random variables. Then $E\left[\dfrac X{X+Y}\right]$ equals
A: $\tfrac12$
B: $\tfrac14$
C: $1$
D: Cannot be determined without the distribution
ANS: A
S: By symmetry (exchangeability) $E\left[\dfrac X{X+Y}\right]=E\left[\dfrac Y{X+Y}\right]$.
S: Their sum is $E\left[\dfrac{X+Y}{X+Y}\right]=1$, so each equals $\tfrac12$.
SC: Exchangeability arguments: $E\left[\dfrac{X_1}{X_1+\cdots+X_n}\right]=\dfrac1n$ for i.i.d. positive $X_i$, whatever the distribution.
TR: Do not write $E(X/(X+Y))=E(X)/E(X+Y)$ — expectation of a ratio is not a ratio of expectations (it only happens to agree here).
TIP: Symmetry/exchangeability solves ratio expectations: $E\left[\dfrac{X_i}{\sum X_j}\right]=\dfrac1n$.
TIP: The positivity condition keeps the denominator away from 0; with symmetric signed variables the expectation may not exist.

Q: If $X\sim$ Poisson$(\lambda)$, then $E\left[\dfrac1{X+1}\right]$ equals
A: $\dfrac{1-e^{-\lambda}}\lambda$
B: $\dfrac1{\lambda+1}$
C: $\dfrac{e^{-\lambda}}\lambda$
D: $\dfrac{1-e^{-\lambda}}{\lambda+1}$
ANS: A
S: $E\left[\dfrac1{X+1}\right]=\sum_{x\ge0}\dfrac{e^{-\lambda}\lambda^x}{(x+1)!}=\dfrac{e^{-\lambda}}\lambda\sum_{y\ge1}\dfrac{\lambda^y}{y!}$.
S: $=\dfrac{e^{-\lambda}}\lambda\left(e^\lambda-1\right)=\dfrac{1-e^{-\lambda}}\lambda$.
SC: Absorb $\tfrac1{x+1}$ into the factorial: $\tfrac{\lambda^x}{(x+1)!}=\tfrac1\lambda\cdot\tfrac{\lambda^{x+1}}{(x+1)!}$, then the sum misses only the $y=0$ term. Binomial analogue: $E\left[\tfrac1{X+1}\right]=\tfrac{1-q^{n+1}}{(n+1)p}$.
TR: $\tfrac1{\lambda+1}=\tfrac1{E(X)+1}$ — Jensen says the true value is larger.
CHK: eq(summation(exp(-lam)*lam**k/factorial(k+1),(k,0,oo)), (1-exp(-lam))/lam)
TIP: $\dfrac1{x+1}\cdot\dfrac{\lambda^x}{x!}=\dfrac1\lambda\cdot\dfrac{\lambda^{x+1}}{(x+1)!}$ — shift the index and the sum becomes $e^\lambda-1$.
TIP: As $\lambda\to0$ the value tends to 1 (then $X=0$ almost surely) — a quick limit check of the options.

Q: $X$ takes the value $(-1)^{k+1}\dfrac{2^k}k$ with probability $2^{-k}$, $k=1,2,3,\dots$. Which statement is correct?
A: $E(X)$ does not exist, although $\sum x_kp_k$ converges (to $\ln2$)
B: $E(X)=\ln2$
C: $E(X)=0$
D: $E(X)=\infty$
ANS: A
S: $\sum x_kp_k=\sum(-1)^{k+1}\tfrac1k=\ln2$ — but only conditionally convergent.
S: Absolute series: $\sum|x_k|p_k=\sum\tfrac1k=\infty$.
S: G&K (§6·1, Remark 1) defines $E(X)$ only when the series converges absolutely, so $E(X)$ does not exist.
SC: Existence of expectation $\iff E|X|<\infty$. A rearrangeable (conditionally convergent) series cannot define a mean.
TR: Option B is the value of the ordered series — the classic trap.
TIP: Compare the 2021 PYQ with values $(-1)^{i+1}\tfrac{2^i}{i!}$: there $\sum|x|p=\sum\tfrac1{i!}<\infty$, so $E(X)=1-e^{-1}$ exists.
TIP: Rearranging a conditionally convergent series can give any sum (Riemann) — that is why absolute convergence is required.
CHK: close(sum((-1)**(j+1)/j for j in range(1,200001)), math.log(2), 1e-5)

Q: For random variables $X,Y$ with finite second moments:<br>1. If $X,Y$ are independent then $E(XY)=E(X)E(Y)$<br>2. If $E(XY)=E(X)E(Y)$ then $X,Y$ are independent<br>3. $E(XY)=E(X)E(Y)$ if and only if $\text{Cov}(X,Y)=0$<br>Which are correct?
A: 1 and 3 only
B: 1 and 2 only
C: 1, 2 and 3
D: 3 only
ANS: A
S: Theorem 6·2 (multiplication theorem): independence gives $E(XY)=E(X)E(Y)$.
S: $\text{Cov}(X,Y)=E(XY)-E(X)E(Y)$, so statement 3 is an identity.
S: Statement 2 is false: $X\sim U(-1,1)$, $Y=X^2$ gives $E(XY)=0=E(X)E(Y)$ but $X,Y$ are dependent.
SC: The product rule characterises <b>uncorrelatedness</b>, not independence.
TIP: Independence gives $E[g(X)h(Y)]=E\,g(X)\,E\,h(Y)$ for <i>all</i> functions $g,h$ — that stronger property is equivalent to independence.
TIP: G&K objective: "$\text{Cov}(X,Y)=0\Rightarrow X,Y$ independent" — FALSE.
TIP: For jointly normal variables, uncorrelated does imply independent.

Q: $f$ and $g$ are both non-decreasing functions and $X$ is a random variable with the relevant expectations finite. Then necessarily
A: $E[f(X)g(X)]\ge E[f(X)]\,E[g(X)]$
B: $E[f(X)g(X)]\le E[f(X)]\,E[g(X)]$
C: $E[f(X)g(X)]=E[f(X)]\,E[g(X)]$
D: no general inequality holds
ANS: A
S: For an independent copy $X'$: $[f(X)-f(X')][g(X)-g(X')]\ge0$ because $f,g$ move in the same direction.
S: Taking expectations: $2E[f(X)g(X)]-2E f(X)\,E g(X)\ge0$.
S: Hence $\text{Cov}(f(X),g(X))\ge0$ (G&K §6·7·1, "another useful inequality").
SC: Same-direction monotone functions of one variable are non-negatively correlated; opposite directions give $\le$.
TIP: Example: $E(Xe^X)\ge E(X)E(e^X)$.
TIP: With $f(x)=x$, $g(x)=-x$ (opposite directions) you get $-E(X^2)\le-[E(X)]^2$, i.e. $\text{Var}(X)\ge0$.
TIP: This is Chebychev's "association" inequality, different from Chebychev's probability inequality.

Q: $A$ and $B$ throw a fair die alternately, $A$ first, for a stake of Rs. 44 which goes to whoever first throws a six. Their respective expectations are
A: Rs. 24 and Rs. 20
B: Rs. 22 and Rs. 22
C: Rs. 26 and Rs. 18
D: Rs. 20 and Rs. 24
ANS: A
S: $P(A\text{ wins})=\tfrac16+\left(\tfrac56\right)^2\tfrac16+\cdots=\dfrac{1/6}{1-25/36}=\dfrac6{11}$.
S: $P(B\text{ wins})=\dfrac5{11}$.
S: Expectations: $44\cdot\tfrac6{11}=24$ and $44\cdot\tfrac5{11}=20$.
SC: Alternating first-success game: first player's chance $=\dfrac{p}{1-q^2}=\dfrac1{1+q}$. With $q=\tfrac56$: $\tfrac6{11}$.
CHK: 44*R(6,11) == 24
TIP: Geometric series for alternating turns: $P(A)=p\sum_{k\ge0}q^{2k}=\dfrac{p}{1-q^2}$.
TIP: The player who throws first always has the advantage; the two expectations must add to the stake (Rs. 44).
TIP: With a coin ($p=\tfrac12$) the first player's chance is $\tfrac23$.

A: $\dfrac{m(n+1)(n-m)}{12}$

C: $\dfrac{m(n+1)(n+m)}{12}$
D: $\dfrac{m(n-1)(n-m)}{12}$

TR: Option B is the with-replacement answer (no correction).

TIP: $E(S)=\dfrac{m(n+1)}2$ is unaffected by replacement.
TIP: Covariance of two draws without replacement: $-\dfrac{\sigma^2}{n-1}$ with $\sigma^2=\dfrac{n^2-1}{12}$.

Q: Consider the zero–one laws:<br>1. Borel's 0–1 law: for independent events $A_n$, $P(\limsup A_n)$ is 0 or 1 according as $\sum P(A_n)$ converges or diverges<br>2. Kolmogorov's 0–1 law: every tail event of a sequence of independent random variables has probability 0 or 1<br>3. For independent $X_n$, the event $\{\bar X_n\text{ converges}\}$ is a tail event<br>Which are correct?
A: 1, 2 and 3
B: 1 and 2 only
C: 2 only
D: 1 and 3 only
ANS: A
S: Statement 1 combines the two Borel–Cantelli lemmas (independence needed for the divergent case).
S: Statement 2 is Kolmogorov's 0–1 law.
S: Changing finitely many $X_n$ does not affect whether $\bar X_n=\tfrac1n\sum X_i$ converges (the finite part divided by $n$ tends to 0), so 3 is a tail event.
SC: Tail events ignore any finite number of terms; for independent sequences they are trivial (probability 0 or 1).
TIP: $\{X_n\to0\}$, $\{\sum X_n\text{ converges}\}$, $\{\limsup X_n>c\}$ are tail events too.
TIP: $\{S_n>0\text{ for infinitely many }n\}$ is not a tail event in general, but it is exchangeable (Hewitt–Savage).
TIP: The SLLN says the tail event $\{\bar X_n\to\mu\}$ has probability 1 (not 0) when $E|X_1|<\infty$.
