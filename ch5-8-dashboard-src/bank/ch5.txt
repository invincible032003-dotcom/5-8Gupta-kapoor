Q: Which of the following is <b>not</b> necessarily a property of the distribution function $F(x)=P(X\le x)$ of a random variable $X$?
A: $F$ is monotonic non-decreasing
B: $F$ is continuous from the right at every point
C: $F(-\infty)=0$ and $F(\infty)=1$
D: $F$ is continuous at every point
ANS: D
S: For $a<b$: $F(b)-F(a)=P(a<X\le b)\ge 0$, so $F$ is non-decreasing — always true.
S: $F(a+0)=\lim_{h\downarrow 0}P(X\le a+h)=P(X\le a)=F(a)$ by continuity of $P$ on decreasing sequences, so $F$ is always right-continuous.
S: The limits $F(-\infty)=0,\ F(\infty)=1$ hold for every distribution function.
S: But $F(a)-F(a-0)=P(X=a)$, which is positive at every mass point of a discrete (or mixed) r.v.; so $F$ need not be continuous (left-continuity fails at jumps).
SC: Memorise the trio: <b>non-decreasing, right-continuous, 0 at $-\infty$ and 1 at $+\infty$</b>. Jump at $a$ $=P(X=a)$. "Continuous everywhere" holds only for continuous r.v.s.
TR: Students confuse right-continuity (always) with left-continuity (fails at atoms).
TIP: A distribution function may jump; it can never decrease and can never be undefined. So any option saying "continuous" or "differentiable everywhere" is the odd one out.
TIP: Right-continuity comes from the event form $\{X\le x\}$: the limit $\bigcap_n\{X\le x+\tfrac1n\}=\{X\le x\}$ keeps the endpoint.
TIP: If the definition were $F(x)=P(X<x)$ (some older texts), $F$ would be left-continuous instead — read the definition in the stem.

Q: For any random variable $X$ with distribution function $F$, $P(a\le X<b)$ equals
A: $F(b)-F(a)$
B: $F(b-0)-F(a-0)$
C: $F(b)-F(a-0)$
D: $F(b-0)-F(a)$
ANS: B
S: $P(X<b)=F(b-0)$ (left limit) and $P(X<a)=F(a-0)$.
S: $\{a\le X<b\}=\{X<b\}\setminus\{X<a\}$, hence $P(a\le X<b)=F(b-0)-F(a-0)$.
S: Check with the four cases: $(a,b]\to F(b)-F(a)$; $[a,b]\to F(b)-F(a-0)$; $(a,b)\to F(b-0)-F(a)$; $[a,b)\to F(b-0)-F(a-0)$.
SC: Rule: an <b>included</b> left end uses $F(a-0)$; an <b>excluded</b> right end uses $F(b-0)$. For a continuous r.v. all four are equal.
TR: Option A is correct only for $(a,b]$ or for a continuous r.v.
TIP: Draw a number line: a solid dot means the end is included, a hollow dot means excluded; take $F(\cdot-0)$ at every included left end and every excluded right end.
TIP: Sanity check with a degenerate law $P(X=a)=1$: $P(a\le X<b)=1$, and only $F(b-0)-F(a-0)=1-0$ gives that.

Q: The distribution function of $X$ is $F(x)=0$ for $x<0$ and $F(x)=1-\tfrac14 e^{-x}$ for $x\ge 0$. Then $P(X=0)$ and $P(X>0)$ are respectively
A: $\tfrac34,\ \tfrac14$
B: $\tfrac14,\ \tfrac34$
C: $0,\ 1$
D: $\tfrac34,\ 1$
ANS: A
S: Jump at $0$: $P(X=0)=F(0)-F(0-0)=\left(1-\tfrac14\right)-0=\tfrac34$.
S: $P(X>0)=1-F(0)=1-\tfrac34=\tfrac14$ (the continuous part carries the remaining mass).
S: So $X$ is a <b>mixed</b> random variable: an atom of $3/4$ at $0$ plus density $\tfrac14e^{-x}$ on $(0,\infty)$.
SC: Mass at a point = height of the jump of $F$ there. Always check $F(0)$ vs $F(0-)$ when a formula "starts" at $0$ with a non-zero value.
TR: Option C treats $X$ as continuous and ignores the jump.
CHK: R(1)-R(1,4) == R(3,4)
TIP: Total mass check: atom $\tfrac34$ at $0$ plus continuous mass $\int_0^\infty\tfrac14e^{-x}dx=\tfrac14$ gives $1$.
TIP: For a mixed law, $E(X)=\sum x\,P(X=x)+\int x\,f(x)\,dx$ — here $0\cdot\tfrac34+\tfrac14\cdot1=\tfrac14$.

Q: $F(x)=0$ for $x<0$; $F(x)=\dfrac{x+1}{4}$ for $0\le x<1$; $F(x)=1$ for $x\ge 1$.<br>1. $P(X=1)=\tfrac12$<br>2. $P(0<X<1)=\tfrac14$<br>3. $X$ is a continuous random variable<br>Which of the above is/are correct?
A: 1 and 2 only
B: 2 and 3 only
C: 1 only
D: 1, 2 and 3
ANS: A
S: Jump at $0$: $F(0)-F(0-)=\tfrac14-0=\tfrac14$, so $P(X=0)=\tfrac14$.
S: Jump at $1$: $F(1)-F(1-)=1-\tfrac24=\tfrac12$, so $P(X=1)=\tfrac12$ — statement 1 true.
S: $P(0<X<1)=F(1-)-F(0)=\tfrac12-\tfrac14=\tfrac14$ — statement 2 true.
S: $F$ has jumps, so $X$ is mixed, not continuous — statement 3 false. (Check: $\tfrac14+\tfrac14+\tfrac12=1$.)
SC: Split total mass = (sum of jumps) + (continuous rise). Here $\tfrac14+\tfrac12$ in jumps and $\tfrac14$ spread uniformly on $(0,1)$.
CHK: R(1,4)+R(1,4)+R(1,2) == 1
TIP: Read every jump as "right value minus left limit": at $x=0$ it is $F(0)-0$, at $x=1$ it is $1-\lim_{x\uparrow1}F(x)$.
TIP: The continuous part has density $F'(x)=\tfrac14$ on $(0,1)$, so its mass is $\tfrac14$; masses $\tfrac14+\tfrac12+\tfrac14=1$ confirm the reading.
TIP: Any $F$ with at least one jump makes $X$ discrete or mixed — statement "$X$ is continuous" is then automatically false.

Q: Consider the following statements about distribution functions:<br>1. The set of discontinuity points of any distribution function is at most countable.<br>2. A distribution function can have at most one jump of size greater than $\tfrac12$.<br>3. A distribution function can have infinitely many jumps each of size at least $0.01$.<br>Which of the above is/are correct?
A: 1 and 2 only
B: 1 only
C: 2 and 3 only
D: 1, 2 and 3
ANS: A
S: Jumps are probabilities of atoms and all jumps add to at most $1$.
S: Number of jumps of size $>1/n$ is less than $n$; taking the union over $n=1,2,\dots$ gives a countable set — statement 1 true.
S: Two jumps each $>\tfrac12$ would total more than $1$ — statement 2 true.
S: Infinitely many jumps each $\ge 0.01$ would total $\infty$ — statement 3 false (at most 100 such jumps).
SC: "Jumps of size $\ge 1/n$: at most $n$." That single counting argument answers every question of this type.
TIP: Count argument: if there were $m$ jumps each at least $\tfrac1n$, then $m\cdot\tfrac1n\le1$, i.e. $m\le n$.
TIP: "At most countable" does not mean "finite": the geometric law has infinitely (countably) many jumps.
TIP: Two jumps of exactly $\tfrac12$ are possible (fair coin); only jumps strictly greater than $\tfrac12$ are limited to one.

Q: $X$ is a continuous random variable with distribution function $F$. What is $P\left[0.2\le F(X)\le 0.7\right]$?
A: $0.5$
B: $0.9$
C: $0.14$
D: Depends on $F$
ANS: A
S: For continuous $F$, $U=F(X)$ satisfies $P(U\le u)=P(X\le F^{-1}(u))=F(F^{-1}(u))=u$, $0<u<1$.
S: Hence $F(X)\sim U(0,1)$ — the probability integral transform.
S: $P(0.2\le U\le 0.7)=0.7-0.2=0.5$.
SC: $P[a\le F(X)\le b]=b-a$ for every continuous $F$ (G&K Exercise 5(d), Q3). No computation needed.
TR: "Depends on $F$" is the trap — the answer is distribution-free.
TIP: $F(X)\sim U(0,1)$ needs $F$ continuous; for a discrete $X$, $F(X)$ takes only the values of $F$ at the atoms.
TIP: Hence $P[F(X)\le u]=u$ and any interval $[a,b]\subset[0,1]$ receives probability $b-a$.

Q: Let $X_1,X_2,\dots$ be random variables on a probability space $(S,\mathcal B,P)$.<br>1. $X_1X_2$ is a random variable.<br>2. $\max(X_1,X_2)$ is a random variable.<br>3. $\sup_n X_n$ is a random variable whenever it is finite for all outcomes.<br>Which of the above are correct?
A: 1 and 2 only
B: 2 and 3 only
C: 1 and 3 only
D: 1, 2 and 3
ANS: D
S: A function $X:S\to\mathbb R$ is a r.v. iff $\{\omega: X(\omega)\le a\}\in\mathcal B$ for every real $a$ (Theorem 5·1).
S: Sums, products and scalar multiples of r.v.s are r.v.s (Theorem 5·2).
S: $\{\max(X_1,X_2)\le a\}=\{X_1\le a\}\cap\{X_2\le a\}\in\mathcal B$ (Theorem 5·5).
S: $\{\sup_n X_n\le a\}=\bigcap_n\{X_n\le a\}\in\mathcal B$ (Theorem 5·3). All three are true.
SC: Measurability is preserved by every "reasonable" operation: $+,\times$, max, min, sup, inf, lim sup, continuous or monotone functions.
TIP: Anything built from countably many random variables by $+,\times,\max,\min,\sup,\inf,\lim\sup$ or by a Borel function is again a random variable.
TIP: The only care point is finiteness: $\sup_nX_n$ must be finite (or you work with extended real-valued variables).
TIP: $\tfrac1X$ is a random variable too once the value at $X=0$ is defined (G&K Theorem 5·4).

Q: If $f_1$ and $f_2$ are probability density functions, then $g(x)=\theta f_1(x)+(1-\theta)f_2(x)$ is a p.d.f. for every such pair $f_1,f_2$ if and only if
A: $\theta$ is any real number
B: $0\le\theta\le 1$
C: $\theta>0$
D: $\theta=\tfrac12$
ANS: B
S: $\int g=\theta+(1-\theta)=1$ for every $\theta$, so normalisation is automatic.
S: Non-negativity is the binding condition: if $\theta>1$, choose $f_1=0$ where $f_2>0$; then $g=(1-\theta)f_2<0$ there. If $\theta<0$ the symmetric argument applies.
S: For $0\le\theta\le1$ both weights are non-negative, so $g\ge0$. Hence $0\le\theta\le1$.
SC: A mixture is a density when weights are <b>non-negative and add to 1</b>. Integration alone never catches negative weights.
TIP: Normalisation of a mixture holds for every $\theta$; the exam trap is to stop there. Always check non-negativity separately.
TIP: The same rule builds mixed distributions: $F=\theta F_{\text{discrete}}+(1-\theta)F_{\text{continuous}}$ with $0\le\theta\le1$.
TIP: For a special pair (e.g. $f_1\le2f_2$) some $\theta$ outside $[0,1]$ may still work — "for every pair" is the key phrase.

Q: A random variable $X$ has the probability distribution $$\begin{array}{c|cccccccc} x&0&1&2&3&4&5&6&7\\\hline p(x)&0&k&2k&2k&3k&k^2&2k^2&7k^2+k\end{array}$$ The value of $k$ is
A: $\tfrac1{10}$
B: $-1$
C: $\tfrac19$
D: $\tfrac15$
ANS: A
S: $\sum p(x)=1\Rightarrow 9k+10k^2=1\Rightarrow 10k^2+9k-1=0$.
S: $(10k-1)(k+1)=0\Rightarrow k=\tfrac1{10}$ or $k=-1$.
S: $k=-1$ makes $p(1)<0$, so it is rejected: $k=\tfrac1{10}$.
SC: Add linear and quadratic parts separately ($9k$ and $10k^2$), solve the quadratic, then reject the root giving negative probabilities.
TR: $-1$ is a genuine root of the equation — it is there to catch students who do not apply $p(x)\ge 0$.
CHK: set(solve(10*k**2+9*k-1,k)) == {R(1,10),-1}
TIP: Group terms by power of $k$ before solving: coefficients of $k$ add to $1+2+2+3+1=9$, of $k^2$ to $1+2+7=10$.
TIP: After finding $k$, quickly verify one probability is non-negative — a negative root is always the planted distractor.

B: $\tfrac18$

Q: A student matches three historical events with three years purely at random. Let $X$ be the number of correct matches.<br>1. $P(X=2)=0$<br>2. $E(X)=1$<br>3. $P(X=0)=\tfrac13$<br>Which of the above are correct?
A: 1 and 2 only
B: 2 and 3 only
C: 1 and 3 only
D: 1, 2 and 3
ANS: D
S: There are $3!=6$ equally likely matchings. Exactly 3 correct: 1 way; exactly 1 correct: 3 ways (fix one, swap the other two); exactly 0 correct: 2 derangements.
S: Exactly 2 correct is impossible (the third is then forced correct), so $P(X=2)=0$.
S: $P(X=0)=\tfrac26=\tfrac13$, $P(X=1)=\tfrac36$, $P(X=3)=\tfrac16$.
