Q: $X$ is a Bernoulli variate with $P(X=1)=p$.<br>1. $E(X^r)=p$ for every $r\ge1$<br>2. $\text{Var}(X)\le\tfrac14$<br>3. $\mu_3=pq(q-p)$<br>Which are correct?
A: 1, 2 and 3
B: 1 and 2 only
C: 2 and 3 only
D: 1 only
ANS: A
S: $X^r=X$ because $X\in\{0,1\}$, so $E(X^r)=p$.
S: $\text{Var}=pq=p(1-p)\le\tfrac14$ with equality at $p=\tfrac12$.
S: $\mu_3=E(X-p)^3=q(-p)^3+p\,q^3=pq(q^2-p^2)=pq(q-p)$.
SC: Bernoulli is $B(1,p)$: every binomial formula with $n=1$. Sums of independent Bernoullis give the binomial.
CHK: expand(q*(-p)**3+p*q**3 - p*q*(q-p)*(q+p)) == 0
TIP: Any power of an indicator is the indicator itself, so all raw moments of a Bernoulli variable equal $p$.
TIP: $\mu_3=pq(q-p)$ vanishes only at $p=\tfrac12$ — the Bernoulli law is symmetric only for a fair coin.
TIP: Scaled version: if the values are $\pm1$ (Remark 7·1), $Y=2X-1$ has mean $p-q$ and variance $4pq$.

Q: A binomial variate has mean 16 and variance 8. Then $P(X\ge1)$ is
A: $1-2^{-32}$
B: $1-2^{-16}$
C: $2^{-32}$
D: $1-32\cdot2^{-32}$
ANS: A
S: $q=\tfrac{\text{var}}{\text{mean}}=\tfrac8{16}=\tfrac12$, $p=\tfrac12$, $n=\tfrac{16}{1/2}=32$.
S: $P(X=0)=q^n=2^{-32}$, so $P(X\ge1)=1-2^{-32}$.
SC: $q=\sigma^2/\mu$, then $n=\mu/p$. Takes five seconds.
CHK: R(8,16) == R(1,2)
TIP: $q=\dfrac{\sigma^2}\mu$ is the fastest route to every binomial parameter question.
TIP: $P(X=0)=q^n$ and $P(X=1)=npq^{n-1}$ — the two probabilities most often asked.
TIP: With $p=\tfrac12$ the law is symmetric: $P(X\ge17)=\tfrac12\left(1-P(X=16)\right)$.

Q: For a binomial distribution the mean is 6 and the standard deviation is $\sqrt2$. The parameters $(n,p)$ are
A: $\left(9,\ \tfrac23\right)$
B: $\left(18,\ \tfrac13\right)$
C: $\left(12,\ \tfrac12\right)$
D: $\left(9,\ \tfrac13\right)$
ANS: A
S: $npq=2,\ np=6\Rightarrow q=\tfrac13,\ p=\tfrac23$.
S: $n=\tfrac6{2/3}=9$.
SC: Use variance, not SD: $q=\sigma^2/\mu=2/6$.
TR: $(18,\tfrac13)$ has mean 6 but variance 4.
CHK: 9*R(2,3)*R(1,3) == 2
TIP: Square the standard deviation before dividing — SD $\sqrt2$ means variance 2.
TIP: $n$ must come out a positive integer; a fractional $n$ means an arithmetic slip.

Q: Which of the following (mean, variance) pairs is impossible for a binomial distribution?
A: mean 3, variance 4
B: mean 4, variance 3
C: mean 6, variance 2
D: mean 2.5, variance 1.25
ANS: A
S: For $B(n,p)$: variance $=$ mean $\times q$ with $0<q<1$, so variance $<$ mean.
S: Mean 3 with variance 4 would need $q=\tfrac43>1$ — impossible.
S: The others give $(n,p)=(16,\tfrac14),(9,\tfrac23),(5,\tfrac12)$ — all valid.
SC: Binomial: $\sigma^2<\mu$; Poisson: $\sigma^2=\mu$; negative binomial: $\sigma^2>\mu$.
TIP: Binomial variance $npq$ is always smaller than the mean $np$ because $q<1$.
TIP: Also $n=\dfrac{\mu^2}{\mu-\sigma^2}$ must be a positive integer — the test used in the 2019 PYQ.
TIP: Poisson allows equality, the negative binomial requires variance larger than mean.

Q: In a binomial distribution with 5 independent trials, the probabilities of 1 and 2 successes are 0.4096 and 0.2048. The parameter $p$ is
A: $0.2$
B: $0.25$
C: $0.4$
D: $0.1$
ANS: A
S: $\dfrac{P(2)}{P(1)}=\dfrac{\binom52p^2q^3}{\binom51pq^4}=\dfrac{2p}q=\dfrac{0.2048}{0.4096}=\dfrac12$.
S: $\dfrac pq=\dfrac14\Rightarrow p=0.2$.
SC: Ratio of successive terms: $\dfrac{P(x+1)}{P(x)}=\dfrac{n-x}{x+1}\cdot\dfrac pq$. Ratios avoid solving high-degree equations.
CHK: 5*R(2,10)*R(8,10)**4 == R(4096,10000)
TIP: Divide successive probabilities: the binomial coefficients and most powers cancel.
TIP: $0.4096=5p(0.8)^4$ with $p=0.2$: $0.8^4=0.4096$ — recognising powers of $0.8$ saves time.
TIP: Once $p$ is known, $P(X=0)=0.8^5=0.32768$.

Q: The probability that a man hits a target is $\tfrac14$. The least number of times he must fire so that the probability of hitting the target at least once exceeds $\tfrac23$ is
A: $4$
B: $3$
C: $5$
D: $6$
ANS: A
S: $1-\left(\tfrac34\right)^n>\tfrac23\iff\left(\tfrac34\right)^n<\tfrac13$.
S: $\left(\tfrac34\right)^3=\tfrac{27}{64}=0.42$, $\left(\tfrac34\right)^4=\tfrac{81}{256}=0.316$. So $n=4$.
SC: "At least once" $\Rightarrow$ solve $q^n<1-P$ by trying powers.
CHK: min(m for m in range(1,20) if 1-R(3,4)**m > R(2,3)) == 4
TIP: "At least once with probability $>P$" $\iff q^n<1-P\iff n>\dfrac{\ln(1-P)}{\ln q}$.
TIP: Round up to the next integer; check the boundary value directly.
TIP: G&K's companion: with $p=\tfrac13$ and target 90%, $n=6$.

Q: A lot contains 1% defective items. The minimum sample size $n$ such that the probability of finding at least one defective is at least 0.95 is
A: $299$
B: $298$
C: $300$
D: $95$
ANS: A

S: $n$ must be an integer, so $n=299$.
SC: Rule of three: $n\approx3/p$ for 95% ($\ln20\approx3$). Here $\approx300$, then refine.
TR: Rounding $298.07$ down to 298 gives probability just below 0.95.
CHK: min(m for m in range(250,350) if 1-0.99**m >= 0.95) == 299
TIP: $\ln0.95$-type calculations: $\ln0.05\approx-3.00$ and $\ln0.99\approx-0.01005$.

B: 2 only
C: Both 1 and 2
D: Neither 1 nor 2
